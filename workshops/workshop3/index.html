The Geopolitics of Compute: The AI Energy Crisis

Software is often portrayed as weightless and borderless, but modern Artificial Intelligence is deeply anchored to physical reality: extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, sovereign island supply chains, and gigawatt-scale power grids. Explore the geopolitics of compute and the impending energy bottleneck confronting modern AI.

Key Themes & Debate Prompts

Each segment of the workshop addresses a critical intersection between artificial intelligence, geopolitical sovereignty, and public infrastructure.

THEME 1

The Invisible Hand vs. Physical Hardware

Silicon Valley was founded upon cyberlibertarian ideals—the premise that software and information should float unbound above state borders and government regulation. Yet as Cathy O'Neil highlights in Weapons of Math Destruction, unmanaged algorithmic systems create deep, invisible structural externalities. While software seeks to be boundless, modern artificial intelligence is fundamentally constrained by physical compute.

💬 Discussion Prompt: "If software claims to be borderless, what happens when the hardware running it depends entirely on sovereign nation-states?"
THEME 2

The Compute Bottleneck & Semiconductor Chokepoints

As chronicled in Chris Miller's Chip War, advanced computing hinges upon severe geographical and industrial chokepoints. Taiwan's TSMC fabricates over 90% of the world's leading-edge AI chips, while the Netherlands' ASML is the sole global manufacturer of Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) photolithography scanners required to print them. To safeguard national security, the United States has enforced export curbs on toolmaking sales to China while deploying billions in CHIPS Act subsidies to construct domestic fabs.

💬 Discussion Prompt: "If 90% of advanced AI chips depend on a single island, is AI leadership fundamentally a computer science problem or a geopolitical one?"
THEME 3

The AI Energy Crisis & Public Grid Consequences

A single generative AI query draws approximately 10 times the electricity of a traditional search engine index lookup. By 2030, global data center energy consumption is projected to reach 945 terawatt-hours—surpassing the annual electrical demand of Japan. In the US, data centers could consume 12% of total national electricity by 2028, driving wholesale electricity price surges of up to 267% near concentrated data hubs and sparking intense community pushback (a recent IPPSR poll showed 70% of Michigan residents oppose new local data centers).

💬 Discussion Prompt: "When AI compute demand drives up public electricity rates, should hyperscalers be legally obligated to build and fund their own independent power grids?"
THEME 4

Big Tech's Nuclear Pivot & Off-Grid Infrastructure

Because solar and wind power generation remain intermittent, tech conglomerates have turned aggressively to baseload nuclear energy. Microsoft executed a landmark 20-year power purchase agreement to restart the dormant Three Mile Island Unit 1 nuclear reactor in Pennsylvania. Concurrently, Google and Amazon AWS are allocating billions into Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) from Kairos Power and Energy Northwest, while frontier startups prototype hydrogen-powered off-grid facilities to bypass utility interconnect delays.

💬 Discussion Prompt: "Should federal and state governments strictly regulate where, how, and with what fuels AI data centers are built to protect national grid reliability?"
